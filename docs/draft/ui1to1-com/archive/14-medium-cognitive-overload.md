## Summary

Alexis Collins (Design Bootcamp, 10 November 2025) distinguishes cognitive overload from classic burnout. The scene is small: staring at a dropdown for half a minute, unable to choose, after an ordinary online day rather than an 80-hour week. The claim is that the exhaustion comes from thinking — triage, switching, and notifications — not only from hours worked.

A “light” morning in the essay: 47 notifications across 12 apps, 23 unread Slack messages in 5 active channels, 156 unread emails, and hundreds of micro-decisions before the job starts. Traditional burnout is overwork, and the remedy is less work. Overload can wreck an eight-hour day because focus never establishes. Collins cites the 23-minute refocus finding, then says the average knowledge worker checks email about every 6 minutes and Slack about every 5, so partial attention is the steady state. Dunbar’s number (about 150 relationships) is the contrast with awareness of hundreds or thousands of people across platforms. Interfaces, in this telling, are built to maximise engagement: badges, pull-to-refresh, algorithmic feeds.

Gloria Mark at UC Irvine is cited for a second result: frequent interruptions make people work faster and produce less, with more stress and frustration. A phone-touch figure of 2,617 times a day stands in for compulsion. Aggravators: unclear priorities, after-hours anxiety, “110+” SaaS apps with different interfaces, and settings nobody configures.

Warning signs are decision freeze, post-meeting exhaustion, rereading without comprehension, dread at the laptop, forgetting, irritability, guilt when not working, and no memory of real focus. The personal protocol that worked for the author: notifications only for critical calls (context switches down about 80%), two offline morning hours, email twice a day moved into a task system, an app audit from 47 down to 12, async by default, one meeting-free day a week, and a full offline day every two weeks.

The last move is aimed at designers. Individual hygiene cannot fix products that treat attention as something to extract. Success metrics could include attention respected, not only daily actives. Digital minimalism, basic phones, and ad-free products are the demand signal.

## How it works

The mechanism is cumulative micro-decisions plus context switches. Each app asks for a judgment. None feels large. Working memory and attention residue never clear because the next ping arrives inside the refocus window. Engagement design makes that loop the default. The remedies are subtraction (notifications, apps, meetings) and a change in what builders optimise. The essay does not specify a product architecture; it specifies a design ethic and a personal operating system.

## Implications for ui1to1.com / ui121

This is the human-cost essay for a single interface. ui1to1.com can talk about one person and one surface without sounding like a feature list: the fatigue is the number of places a decision can hide. Collins’ cut from 47 apps to 12 is a consumer version of the same move. A universal canvas goes further for work: the dropdown moment — the actual choice — happens once, in context, instead of after a tour of inboxes.

Designers are the audience of the close, which fits a UI company. The line to take is that every feature either respects attention or spends it. JSONUI should be marketed as a calm, task-shaped interface: show the decision, not a feed. Human-in-the-loop belongs here as a designed pause with everything needed on one screen, not as another notification. Do not adopt the phone-touch or 110-app figures as ui121 data; they are the author’s citations inside a personal essay.
