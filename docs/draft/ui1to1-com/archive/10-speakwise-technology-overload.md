## Summary

Speakwise’s 12 March 2026 roundup (updated 27 August 2026) collects twenty statistics on tool sprawl, app fatigue, and SaaS saturation. The opening cluster is the one marketers repeat: workers toggle between apps about 1,200 times a day, the average company deploys 101 SaaS applications, employees lose 44 hours a year to tool fatigue, and organisations waste about $21 million a year on unused licenses. The piece frames the harm as cognitive, not only financial: every toggle breaks concentration, every extra dashboard is another thing to learn, and deep work disappears.

The notes below keep the claims as Speakwise states them, with the upstream source they name. They are secondary citations. Okta’s 2025 Businesses at Work report is their source for 101 apps on average (under 90 from 2019–2023, then +9% year over year) and 131 at companies over 5,000 employees. A 2022 Harvard Business Review study of three Fortune 500s is the 1,200 toggles source: nearly four hours a week, about 9% of work time, roughly 200 hours a year per person. Cornell and Lokalise, via Fast Company, supply 51 minutes a week (44 hours a year) of tool fatigue, 22% losing more than two hours a week, 56% saying tools hurt every week, and the worst culprits as email (47%), messaging (35%), video (22%), and calendars (17%). Qatalog and Cornell’s Ellis Idea Lab: 9.5 minutes to regain flow after an app switch, 45% less productive, 43% fatigued, about 59 minutes a day searching. Asana’s Anatomy of Work: 60% of time on “work about work,” leaving 40% for the job, with workers moving among 10 apps 25 times a day. Zylo 2025: $21 million average waste, SaaS spend per employee $4,830 (up 21.9%), and earlier duplicate counts of 15 training apps, 11 project tools, and 10 collaboration apps.

Further figures they stack: RingCentral, two-thirds toggling up to 10 times an hour, 31% losing their train of thought, and up to an hour a day just among communication apps (32 days a year; a 2023 update they cite says the equivalent of 62 days). Microsoft Work Trend Index: 275 interruptions a day, about one every two minutes; 117 emails and 153 Teams messages; 60% of time in communication; 48% of employees and 52% of leaders calling work chaotic and fragmented. McKinsey: 1.8 hours a day (9.3 a week, about 480 a year) searching for information. 55% say multiple platforms do the same job, and 79% say the company has not consolidated. Gartner: global SaaS near $300 billion in 2025, with about 30% “toxic” (~$90 billion). Rensselaer, via LumApps: information overload about $1 trillion a year worldwide. UC Irvine: 23 minutes 15 seconds to fully refocus after a significant interruption. 17% switch more than 100 times a day. Context switching equated to five working weeks a year. Atlassian State of Teams 2026 (12,035 workers and 173 Fortune 1000 executives): an “AI fragmentation tax” of about $161 billion a year for the Fortune 500; 85% use AI, 29% have it in the daily workflow. Omnissa 2026: almost 24 minutes to refocus; AI assistant use up nearly 1,000% year over year. Zylo 2026: 78% of IT leaders hit by unexpected AI or consumption charges; AI-native spend +108% (+393% above 10,000 employees); business units control 81% of SaaS spend, IT 15%; 61% cut other projects to absorb it.

The close is their product pitch: speak once, transcribe into Notion, add no new dashboard. The research value is the statistic stack, not that pitch.

## Key facts

- Headline quartet: ~1,200 toggles/day; ~101 SaaS apps/company; ~44 hours/year tool fatigue; ~$21 million unused licenses.
- Refocus: 9.5 minutes after an app switch (Qatalog/Cornell); 23 minutes 15 seconds after a full interruption (UC Irvine); ~24 minutes in Omnissa 2026 telemetry.
- Work about work: Asana 60%; McKinsey ~1.8 hours/day searching.
- Fragmentation mood: 48% of employees and 52% of leaders (Microsoft).
- AI layer: Atlassian $161 billion Fortune 500 fragmentation tax; only 29% have embedded AI in daily work.
- Consolidation gap: 79% say the company has done nothing; 55% see overlapping platforms.

## How it works

The article is a citation survey, not a mechanism. The causal story it repeats is digital friction: each adopted tool solved a local problem, nobody retired the previous tool, information and notifications multiplied, and attention residue from interruptions made the next hour shallower. AI tools are described as a new layer of the same pattern, not an automatic cure, because outputs do not land in one workflow.
