## Summary

André Ripla’s LinkedIn essay, published 19 February 2025, argues that workplace digitisation has passed a tipping point: the tools bought to raise output now produce digital fatigue, lower productivity, and burnout. He defines digital fatigue (also technostress or cyber fatigue) as mental exhaustion from overusing devices and platforms, showing up as overwhelm, resistance to the next rollout, and a sense of being behind. The thesis is that over-adoption fails for five reasons: cognitive overload, time spent navigating rather than working, endless retraining, information silos and duplicate effort, and disengagement.

The historical spine runs from mainframes and word processors, through the PC and email, ERP/CRM, mobile and cloud, to the SaaS collaboration wave (Slack, Teams, Zoom) and AI inside those tools. Each wave delivered real speed. The cumulative effect is dozens of interfaces and constant cloud updates. He inventories the modern stack in twelve buckets — chat, office suites, CRM, ERP, HRIS, BI, file storage, design tools, industry software, security, time tracking, portfolio tools — and notes overlap (Slack and Teams both doing chat and files) plus shadow IT and remote-work acceleration.

He balances the promise. Automation, search, analytics, and mobile work are real gains. He cites McKinsey Global Institute on social technologies lifting interaction-worker productivity 20–25%, and Deloitte on digitally strong leadership correlating with better financial performance. The paradox section then cites Workfront (42% saying the number of tools makes them less productive; knowledge workers spending about 40% of the week on primary duties), a Journal of Experimental Psychology finding of up to 50% more errors when people switch tasks often, and IDC on about 2.5 hours a day (about 30% of the day) searching for information. Later sections cover resistance to new software (Gartner: lack of digital dexterity blocking adoption in 54% of organisations surveyed) and overlapping features as “too much of a good thing.”

The page title in the live HTML is “How Over-Tooling Kills Employee Productivity,” slightly tighter than the pulse slug’s “engagement” wording. It reads as a long essay with case-style argument rather than a product page.

Section outline: definition and thesis; history of workplace computing; today’s tool categories; the productivity promise; symptoms; the paradox (time, errors, search); resistance and training; overlap and silos; what streamlining would require.

## How it works

Over-tooling works as a slow accumulation. A company adds a tool per function, employees learn a login per tool, data splits, and the next update resets muscle memory. Productivity math then goes negative because search, switching, and training consume the hours the tool was meant to save. Ripla’s implied fix is a smaller, intentional stack with training, not a named platform.
