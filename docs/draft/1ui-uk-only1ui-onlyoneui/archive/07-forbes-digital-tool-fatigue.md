Title and date match the Forbes listing: “Digital Tool Fatigue: How Too Many Apps Hurt Work and Well-Being,” 4 October 2025.

Robinson’s thesis is that digital tool fatigue — too many tools, alerts, and duplicate platforms — is now a mental-health and productivity problem, and that AI has made an older overload worse. He points back to a 2023 Forbes piece in which 96% of workers in one study said digital tools were not helping them keep up. Workers wanted one solution for many tasks and wanted AI to take a communication chore. Three years later, he says the stack is larger, not calmer.

The new evidence is a Lokalise survey of 1,000 US professionals across 11 industries. Findings he lists: 79% say the company has done nothing to reduce tool fatigue; 60% feel pressure to answer notifications after hours; one in five lose two or more hours a week to switching (over 100 hours, or 2.5 work weeks, a year); 17% switch tabs, apps, or platforms more than 100 times a day; 45% say their tools hinder productivity; the average worker loses 51 minutes a week (about 44 hours a year); 56% say toggling, alerts, and redundant platforms hurt their work each week; over half say collaboration suffers because the stack is disjointed.

He then names two roots: AI and mistrust. A Harvard Business Review account of “work slop” is tied to an MIT Media Lab claim that 95% of organisations see no measurable AI return. Asana’s 2025 Global State of AI at Work is cited for 70% weekly AI use, 84% digital exhaustion, 77% unmanageable workloads, and only 29% of organisations successfully scaling AI. Zach Giglio is paraphrased as saying vendor pressure produces rushed, fragmented rollouts, and overwhelmed people fall back on old habits that rarely include AI. The prescription is organisational: autonomy for employees plus leader-written guidance on tools, norms, and how AI should serve strategy.

## How it works

Robinson is a commentator synthesising surveys, not describing a product. The mechanism is cognitive: each extra platform adds logins, alerts, and duplicate places to look, which chips at focus and collaboration. AI worsens it when companies automate a broken process (“automating chaos”) or ship another copilot without training, so people hide usage or ignore the tool. The fix he endorses is policy and training, not a named platform.
